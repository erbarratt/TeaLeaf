HOW THE GUARDS IN THIEF VR NOTICE YOU - EXPLAINER SCRIPT
=========================================================

Narration in plain text. Visual suggestions in [brackets].


[Opening: a dark street at night, a guard walking a slow loop with a torch.]

In a stealth game, the whole fun depends on one question: does the guard know
I'm here? Too easy, and there's no tension. Too hard, and it feels unfair. So
how does a game decide what a guard can see and hear? Let's look at how Thief
VR does it.


[Scene 1: two bars appear above the guard's head, one yellow, one red.]

Every guard carries two invisible gauges, like the fuel gauge in a car.

The first is SUSPICION. It's the guard thinking, "Hm, was that something?"
When it fills, the guard stops, turns and looks.

The second is DETECTION. That's the guard thinking, "That's definitely a
person." When it fills, the guard raises the alarm and comes after you.

The two gauges empty at different speeds. Suspicion fades quickly, about a
second after the guard stops noticing anything. Detection sticks around: the
guard stays wary for about four seconds, then slowly calms down. That's why a
guard who nearly caught you doesn't instantly forget.


[Scene 2: seen from above, four shapes fan out from the guard's head like
searchlight beams.]

So what fills the gauges? Mostly, what the guard sees.

Human sight isn't one simple cone, and neither is this guard's. Its vision is
split into four zones:

- Right in front of it, about three metres. Anything here is spotted almost
  at once.
- Close by, about six metres, in a wide arc.
- Straight ahead, far away: a beam about sixty degrees wide that becomes a
  ten-metre-wide corridor, reaching about thirty metres. Like
  staring down a corridor.
- The corner of its eye: a very wide zone, also reaching thirty metres. It
  can only pick out things that are well lit.

Each zone has its own rulebook, and the rules change with distance. A
designer has written out, zone by zone and step by step, exactly how fast the
gauges fill.


[Scene 3: the player steps from a pool of torchlight into shadow. The light
around them switches between "lit" and "dark".]

The biggest factor is LIGHT.

The level designers have placed invisible bubbles and cones of "light" by
hand around every torch and lamp. The prologue level alone has sixty-seven.
If you're inside one, you count as lit. Outside, you count as dark. There's
nothing in between.

And the difference is huge:

- Lit: a guard can spot you from thirty metres.
- Dark: past about six metres, you're invisible. Out of the corner of its
  eye, you're invisible at any distance. But come within one or two metres,
  and even darkness won't save you. The guard spots you in a fraction of a
  second.


[Scene 4: the player creeps slowly past a guard, then breaks into a run and
the red bar shoots up.]

The second factor is HOW YOU MOVE.

Standing still makes you harder to notice. Crouching helps too. But moving
fast, especially in the dark near a guard, gives you away almost instantly.
Sprinting past a guard makes the game up to fifty times quicker to spot you.

The lesson to the player is simple: in the shadows, go slowly.


[Scene 5: five little dots on the player's body, head and waist, light up as
the guard looks at them.]

The game doesn't treat you as a single point. It checks five spots: four
around your head and one at your waist. Each counts for a different amount,
and your body counts most. Poke just the top of your head round a corner, and
the guard probably won't notice. Step out with your whole body, and it will.


[Scene 6: footsteps on different floors, with rings rippling out of different
sizes.]

Then there's HEARING.

Every sound in the game falls into one of five volume steps, from silent to
very loud. Each step travels a set distance: two metres, five, ten, up to
fifteen.

The floor matters. Carpet is quiet. Most floors are average. Glass and loose
wooden boards are loud.

And sound has to travel the way a person would walk, round corners and
through doorways. It never goes straight through a wall. A guard on the other
side of a thick wall won't hear you, even if you're only a step away.

One unusual touch: the game can listen to your real voice through the
headset's microphone. Shout in real life, and the guards hear it.


[Scene 7: a torch goes out. A guard turns, frowns, walks over and relights
it.]

Guards don't just watch for you. They notice when things are wrong:

- a torch that's gone out,
- a fellow guard lying unconscious,
- a guard who's been hurt.

When a guard spots one of these, it goes to look. With a torch, it relights
it, undoing your hard work.


[Scene 8: one guard shouts. A second guard nearby turns its head.]

Guards also talk to each other. A muttered remark carries a few metres. A
shout carries about fifteen. Guards within about ten metres of an alarm join
in.


[Scene 9: the player ducks behind a pillar. The guard searches, checking
different spots.]

Finally, what happens when you get away?

If the guard catches sight of you again within a second and a half, it's
straight back on your trail. Losing it properly takes longer: it stays on
high alert for about nine seconds and keeps hunting for up to thirty.

When it searches, it doesn't wander at random. It picks likely spots near
where it last saw you. It also checks its surroundings before looking around,
so it never ends up staring at a blank wall.


[Closing: back to the quiet street. The guard resumes its loop.]

So that's the whole system:

- two gauges, suspicion and detection,
- filled by what the guard sees, depending on light, distance and how you
  move,
- and by what it hears, travelling realistically through the building,
- plus guards who notice things out of place and warn each other.

None of it is magic. It's a big, carefully tuned set of rules, written so
that the player always feels one thing: if I was caught, it was my own fault.

[Fade to black.]
