TeaLeaf Development Log
========================

This file tracks development progress on TeaLeaf, a Thief-style VR stealth game
(Unity, OpenXR + Input System; XR Interaction Toolkit until 2026-09-30).
Newest updates are at the top.


2026-10-10, fourth part (controls, the shoulder pack, the crossbow)
------------------------------------------------------------------
- The maintainer's designs, built the same day. Only the crossbow has
  been tried in the headset; the rest compiles and is untried.
- Controls.
  - Left stick moves, and its click toggles sprint. Right stick turns,
    up mantles, down toggles crouch (PlayerInputXR.ReadCrouchStick: past
    0.7 and more down than sideways, then not again until back above
    0.4). A jumps. B is a universal cancel. X, Y, Menu and the right
    stick's click are unassigned.
  - Handedness is one setting, PlayerInputXR.leftHanded: off, the
    lockpicks are on the left hand and the crossbow on the right; on,
    the other way round. The lock puzzle is never mirrored.
- Aimed throwing: B cancels the aim, and the prop stays in the hand.
  No pitch limits. An arc that lands on nothing is still red, and
  letting go then throws nothing.
- The pack has no button. Either hand reaching over a shoulder (a ball
  beside and behind the head, measured with the head's tilt ignored)
  and pressing grip brings it out on that hand. It stays out while the
  grip is held. The other hand loads it and takes from it.
  PlayerPack.TickSummon() runs before the grab systems, and holding
  the pack is a hand use of its own (HandUse.Pack). The keyring is
  carried by whichever hand isn't holding the pack.
- Loot is worth its coin level: one, two or three gold. The pack shows
  its gold as a number, drawn with TextMeshPro, written only when the
  gold changes. TextMeshPro needs its font and shader imported once
  from the editor (Window > TextMeshPro > Import TMP Essential
  Resources); without them there is no number and a warning says why.
- The crossbow, first version (Player.PlayerCrossbow), tried and
  working.
  - Always worn on the back of one hand, as the lockpicks are on the
    other. It becomes active when grip is pressed with that hand empty,
    pointing at nothing it could grab, and palm down; it stays active
    until grip is let go. A grab always wins over it.
  - Active, it shows an arc like an aimed throw's, flatter and longer.
    A bolt's speed comes from a range set in the editor (how far it
    carries over level ground at the best angle), worked out once at
    load: speed = the square root of range x gravity.
  - The trigger shoots on the press. It is clockwork: a second to
    rewind.
  - Bolts are a pool of eight, made at load. A bolt isn't a physics
    object: it's moved along the same sum the arc was drawn from, so it
    lands where the arc showed, with one physics ray a frame in case
    something has moved into its way.
  - Player.BallisticArc holds the arc maths, shared with aimed
    throwing.
  - To come: choosing the bolt type by gripping the wheel at the back
    of the crossbow with the other hand and turning it; the water,
    noisemaker and rope bolts and their models; ammunition.
- No weapon menu. How the blackjack is brought out is undecided.
- Bug: the crossbow came up with the palm facing up, not down. The
  right hand model is the left one mirrored (x scale -1), and
  Transform.TransformDirection() ignores scale, so on the right hand
  it gave the back of the hand. TransformVector() takes the mirroring
  into account. The same change was made to the direction the crossbow
  shoots.
  - Lesson: for a direction under a mirrored hand, TransformVector()
    and normalise - never TransformDirection() or .forward.
- Bug: "Action count in old and new state must be the same", logged by
  the Input System when the controllers were next detected. The input
  actions file had been edited with Unity open; the editor re-imported
  it but its running input state still had the old number of actions.
  The file was valid. Restarting Unity clears it.
  - Lesson: after editing InputSystem_Actions.inputactions outside
    the editor, restart Unity.
- Bug: the crossbow didn't appear at all. Nothing adds PlayerCrossbow
  to the Hands object; it has to be added by hand.
- A warning seen in passing: three point lights in the scene have
  shadows on, so Unity shrinks eighteen shadow maps to fit its 2048
  atlas. Nothing breaks.
- Lessons from the editing itself.
  - A perl script in a shell heredoc lost the $" at the start of a C#
    interpolated string, and "||" inside a "|"-delimited replacement
    ends it early. Write the script to a file with "#" as the
    delimiter.
  - Compiling outside the editor (Unity's csc.dll on a copy of
    Library/Bee's .rsp file) caught the first of those before Unity
    saw it. New source files appended to the .rsp need a newline
    first: the file doesn't end with one.
- To try in the headset: the shoulder grab, and whether it gets in the
  way of grabbing a ledge behind the head; the pack in the right hand;
  crouch on the stick; B to cancel a throw; the gold number; haptics.
- Next: the crossbow's wheel and bolt types, or the guards (Phase 6).


2026-10-10, third part (docs cut down, a project review, a clean-up)
-------------------------------------------------------------------
- Two rules added to the root CLAUDE.md.
  - "Choosing a solution": every solution is the simplest, most stable
    and most performant way to do the thing; where there are options or
    levels of complexity, lay out the trade-offs and wait for a choice
    before building anything.
  - The CLAUDE.md files, the roadmap and this log describe what is true
    now. No notes that something changed, was decided on a date or
    replaced an earlier plan.
- The roadmap and the five CLAUDE.md files hold the current state only.
  A finished roadmap item is one line; open items keep their detail.
  The roadmap is 31 KB.
- Spatialiser and reverb are done, tuning included.
- A review of the whole project for simpler, more stable or faster ways
  of doing things, from the settings and a search of the scripts. The
  largest finds were in the PC render settings, which were Unity's
  template defaults. The scripts were clean: no scene searches per
  frame, no allocating physics queries.
- PC render settings (PC_RPAsset, PC_Renderer): ambient occlusion
  switched off (a full-screen effect with its own depth pass), no depth
  texture, no opaque texture, HDR off, two shadow cascades with hard
  edges. The shadow distance stays 50m: shorter, and distant shadows
  would vanish while the gameplay light still counted them.
- Packages removed: Visual Scripting, Timeline, Multiplayer Center,
  Version Control, Test Framework. Nothing used them.
- Template leftovers removed: the sample volume profile, and the Look,
  Attack, Interact, Previous and Next input actions.
  - The UI action map stays: the Input System reports an error in the
    editor if the project-wide actions have none. Its keyboard, gamepad,
    touch and joystick schemes stay with it.
  - Lesson: a regex that matches "a block with no braces inside" misses
    input bindings whose path has braces, like {PrimaryAction}. Two
    were left behind and found by checking the file as JSON afterwards.
- SoundPlayer does the one-off sounds and hands the rest to three plain
  classes it makes and ticks: SoundLoopPlayer, ListenerReverb and
  RoomAmbience. Every setting stays on the Sound Player, so the scene's
  tuning is kept.
- SoundRoom works its box out once in Play Mode, not on every lookup.
  In Edit Mode it still works it out each
  time, so rooms can be moved while building.
- A one-off sound's end time is worked out when it starts, so the
  spatialiser no longer asks every voice every frame whether it is
  playing.
- PlayerHaptics sends pulses through each hand's XR device, found once
  and kept. Sending through an input action builds a name on every
  call, and lockpicking pulses twenty times a second. Not yet tried in the
  headset; the LeftHaptic and RightHaptic actions are unused until it
  is.
- Checked: the game scripts compile, with Unity's own compiler run
  outside the editor on the editor's own list of sources and
  references, and then in the editor. Nothing in this part was run in
  the headset.
  - Lesson: Unity keeps each compile's arguments in
    Library/Bee/artifacts/<name>.dag/Assembly-CSharp.rsp. Copying it,
    adding new files and running the editor's csc.dll on it checks code
    Unity hasn't imported yet.
- To check in the headset: the look without ambient occlusion and with
  hard shadow edges; that haptics still buzz; that loops, reverb and
  ambience sound as before.
- Next: as before - the open inventory decisions, then the tools
  (Phase 5) or the guards (Phase 6).


2026-10-10, second part (the target changes: PCVR on a 3060-class rig)
----------------------------------------------------------------------
- No code this part: a decision, and the documents brought into line.
- The maintainer changed what the game is built for. It was "PCVR
  first, then Quest 3 standalone as the real target". It is now PCVR:
  90 fps with 25% headroom, at about 2000 x 2000 pixels per eye, on an
  RTX 3060 12 GB, a Ryzen 5 5600X class processor and 16 GB of RAM.
  That is a budget of 8.3 ms a frame for the CPU and for the GPU.
  Quest 3 standalone is an option for the future, not a target.
- How the rig was chosen. Valve's September 2026 Steam Hardware Survey
  has the RTX 5070 as the single most common card (5.86% of systems),
  32 GB as the most common memory (42.2%; 16 GB 37.8%, 8 GB 6.5%) and
  8 cores as the most common processor (30.1%). The target was briefly
  that rig, then dropped: the RTX 5060, 4060 and 3060 are each weaker
  and together more common than the 5070, so a game that only just
  fits on a 5070 misses on them. The maintainer settled on "3060
  upwards", to cover most Steam players. The maintainer named the card;
  the processor class and the 16 GB carry over from their first
  assumption and are open to change.
  - The survey covers all Steam users, not VR owners (under 2% of
    them), and names no processor models, only core counts. Quest 3 is
    the most used headset on Steam (26.9%), which is the dev setup.
- The performance rules in the root CLAUDE.md were rewritten for it.
  Loosened, because they only existed for mobile hardware: torches may
  cast realtime shadows from moving things; the procedural cobblestone
  and brick shaders are acceptable, subject to measuring; 4x MSAA is
  wanted; an optional post-process effect that can be switched off is
  allowed once measured. Kept, because they are cheap now and costly to
  retrofit, and keep a Quest port possible: no per-frame allocations,
  nothing created during play, costly CPU work sliced, static geometry
  that batches and culls, batcher-friendly stereo shaders, and no
  dependence on a full-screen effect.
- Torch lighting, agreed but not built or tested: Mixed lights in
  Shadowmask mode. The light itself stays realtime, so it can be put
  out; the level's shadows are baked; only moving things are drawn
  into a torch's shadow map. Indirect light at zero, because a torch's
  baked bounce light can't be removed when it goes out. At most four
  such lights overlap at one spot. The realtime shadows are to be a
  quality setting that can be turned off.
- Roadmap: the "Performance rules" block is rewritten; Phase 10 is now
  "Performance pass (PCVR target rig)", starting with finding a way to
  measure on a 3060-class machine, and its Quest-only tasks (standalone
  build, foveated rendering, ASTC, spacewarp) moved to a "possible
  later port" list at its end; Phase 8's lighting items are updated
  for the torch plan; milestone M6 is renamed.
- Open: nothing has been measured on a 3060-class machine, so every
  cost is a judgement until one is found or a scaling from the dev
  machine is worked out.
- Next: as before - the open inventory decisions, then the tools
  (Phase 5) or the guards (Phase 6).


2026-10-10 (sound: loops, reverb, ambience, our own spatialiser)
----------------------------------------------------------------
- Back to the sound system, to finish Phase 2's sound block. All four
  parts below were heard working by the maintainer.
- Looping sounds. Core.SoundLoop goes on the object that makes the
  sound (a torch, a fire) with a cue. SoundPlayer works each loop's
  route out again on the loop's own timer (0.2s in earshot, 1s beyond,
  each a little random so they never share a frame for long) and plays
  the ones that reach the player on a separate pool of 8 voices, so a
  burst of one-off sounds can't cut a torch off. With more in earshot
  than voices the loudest win. In the same room a loop is heard exactly
  where it is; through a portal, and for a moment after changing
  between the two, where it's heard from glides to the new answer.
  Disabling the component fades it out. SoundPlayer has an Update() of
  its own for this (it had none).
- Reverb, to the maintainer's rule: it belongs to the room the listener
  is in, not the sound's. Every sound heard while standing in an echoey
  room gets that room's reverb. Core.ReverbSettings is four numbers
  (level, decay time, damping, size) on every SoundRoom, with presets
  on the component's right-click menu, and one on SoundPlayer for
  outside. One reverb for everything, blended over 0.6s on a change of
  room. A room tuned in the Inspector while playing is heard at once.
- Bug: no reverb at all, whatever the settings. The first version was a
  Unity reverb zone riding on the listener, with each voice sending
  into it. Loops and ambience worked; the reverb was silent. Meta's
  spatialiser plugin was in use at the time, and the working theory -
  not proven - is that a sound passed through a spatialiser plugin
  never reaches Unity's reverb zones. Fix: an AudioReverbFilter on the
  object the AudioListener is on, which works on the whole mix after
  everything else. The price: the ambience gets the reverb too, and
  there is no per-sound send.
  - Lesson: the reverb zone's level settings are whole numbers and the
    reverb filter's are not; the first compile failed on it.
- Room ambience: a background loop with no position, per SoundRoom,
  and one on SoundPlayer for outside. Two plain sources crossfade on a
  change of room; rooms that share a cue carry on without a restart.
- The spatialiser. The Meta XR Audio SDK went in first, as the usual
  one for a Quest game, and worked. The maintainer then asked for
  something platform-agnostic (Meta's is under the Oculus SDK License),
  and after weighing Steam Audio chose to write a simple one. The Meta
  package, its registry and the files it generated were removed the
  same day; no third-party audio package is installed.
  - Core.SpatialVoice, one per voice, changes the voice's samples on
    Unity's audio thread: the far ear hears the sound up to 0.7ms late,
    quieter and duller (the head is in the way), and a sound from
    behind is a little duller in both. It also does the fade with
    distance, since the voice's AudioSource is now plain 2D. The
    direction is kept up every frame as the head turns, which is most
    of how front and behind are told apart.
  - No cue for up and down: that takes measured ear data. Accepted -
    the rooms and portals already keep a guard upstairs from being
    heard through the floor. Steam Audio is the fallback, through
    SoundPlayer's Plugin mode.
  - Core.SpatialiserSettings on SoundPlayer holds its numbers, read
    every frame so they tune by ear in Play Mode. SpatialiserMode
    picks Unity's panning, ours (the default) or a plugin.
- Placeholder sounds: a generated torch crackle, wind and room tone,
  each made to loop without a click. The Sound Test House builder now
  gives its torches a SoundLoop, each room a different reverb, and
  ambience. The L key sound report lists the spatialiser, the room's
  reverb and every loop.
- Small things: a bash command containing a long perl script failed on
  quoting twice; writing the script to a file first worked. Rider's
  code analysis can check a file for compile errors while Unity is in
  the background and hasn't recompiled.
- Not done: the spatialiser's and the reverb presets' numbers are
  first guesses, untuned; the spatialiser's cost on Quest is
  unprofiled; real loop and ambience recordings; one call that puts a
  torch out everywhere (light, flame and loop together) stays with the
  water bolt in Phase 5.
- Roadmap: the three sound items in Phase 2 are ticked, with the tuning
  and profiling left under them.
- Next: as before - the open inventory decisions, then the tools
  (Phase 5) or the guards (Phase 6).


2026-10-09, third part (the pack, loot, keys, the hand state)
-------------------------------------------------------------
- Phase 4 started. Everything below was tested in the headset by the
  maintainer.
- A false start: a hip pocket, built to the roadmap's old plan (loot let
  go of at the waist is pocketed for a running total). The maintainer
  then gave the real design, and the pocket was removed the same day.
  Its way of noticing a release was kept: it never touched how props
  are carried, only compared what each hand held with the frame before.
- The maintainer's design: a backpack that only holds so much, so the
  player keeps the most valuable things and leaves the rest. Phase 4 of
  the roadmap is rewritten for it.
  - Inventory.Pack: summoned with X on the left controller (the
    ButtonX placeholder action renamed Pack), rides on the left hand,
    collides with nothing. A board with a space for the objective and
    one for the keyring, and a 3 x 3 grid whose top left space is for
    gold.
  - Inventory.Loot: a value, a coin level (1-3), and whether it's
    gold or the objective. Gold put anywhere in the pack adds to the
    gold space and is gone.
  - Each piece of loot takes exactly one space, chosen by holding it
    over that space: a free space lights up, a taken one doesn't.
    First built with items taking more than one space, snapping to the
    nearest free space; the maintainer changed both.
  - In the pack an item shrinks to fit its space; taken out, it grows
    back first and then the hand picks it up as normal
    (PlayerHandHolding.TryPickUp, new).
  - Player.LootWorthMarker: one to three coins hover over loot a hand
    carries. Drawn on top, like the reticles.
  - Inventory.PlayerInventory holds the totals, tools owned and bolt
    counts. Data only.
- Bug, found in the headset: an item taken out of the pack sometimes
  stayed small in the hand, fell through everything when dropped, and
  could then be pulled out of its old space from anywhere in the level.
  One cause. The pack switched the item's colliders back on a frame
  before handing it to the hand. For that frame it was an ordinary prop
  under the hand's ray with grip held, so the normal pick-up took it
  first; the pack then saw its own hand-over fail and "put back" an item
  that was in the hand. Fix: the item stays out of physics until the
  moment of the hand-over, which runs after the normal pick-up each
  frame.
  - Lesson: when one system hands an object to another, don't leave it
    for even a frame in a state a third system will act on.
- Keys, to the maintainer's design.
  - Inventory.Key: a coloured pickup, put into the pack like loot. It
    goes onto the keyring (Inventory.Keyring), shown as a coloured bar.
  - Interaction.KeyLock marks a keyed door's lock. The keyring is
    taken from the pack and held to it: with the right key on the ring
    it snaps in, and either hand turns the key a quarter turn with the
    wrist. Anticlockwise if the lock is on the right of the door leaf
    from the player's side, clockwise if on the left.
  - Player.PlayerKeys is the player's half. The Door Test Area's keyed
    door now has a lock and a red key on a stand beside it.
- The hand state. Five systems can have a hand (climbing, carrying,
  doors, lockpicks, keys), and each held a reference to every other and
  checked them all: twenty checks kept in step by hand. Replaced by
  Player.PlayerHandState, which every system asks "does anyone else have
  this hand?". It keeps no state - it reads the systems' own flags when
  asked - so behaviour is unchanged, and a sixth system is one entry
  there. It adds itself to the Hands object, so the scene needed no
  change.
- Small things: Grabbable.Stow() / Unstow() take a prop out of the
  world and bring it back (for the pack). The pack and keyring reuse the
  lockpicking mesh builder and shader. sed in place sometimes fails on
  this Dropbox folder ("Device or resource busy") part way through a
  batch: check what was applied.
- Still to settle: what each coin level is worth; a number for the
  gold; whether tools and bolts go in the pack or keep a wrist menu.
- Next: those decisions, then the tools (blackjack, crossbow) in Phase
  5, or the guards in Phase 6.


2026-10-09, second part (lockpicking built, door leftovers)
-----------------------------------------------------------
- Reported by the maintainer at the start: the smithy looks and climbs
  as intended, the cobblestone crevice shadow looks right, and the door
  push stutter fix works. A texture for the door leaf was dropped from
  the roadmap.
- Lockpicking, the two-pick design from earlier today. Built in one go
  and working in the headset first time.
  - Interaction.PickableLock: marks a lock that can be picked (the
    same object as the door's keyhole). Pickable from either side.
    Works without a door too, for chests later.
  - Interaction.BigLock: the large copy of the lock, and the puzzle

    angle of the hand round the lock's middle, measured from the
    middle of the pick's range and limited to it, so there is nothing
    to remember between frames and no trouble where angles wrap round.
  - Player.PlayerLockpicking: the picks (one object: on the left
    hand, in the right hand, or in a lock) and the hands. Runs before
    climbing, carrying and doors each frame, and they leave a hand
    alone while it's busy with the picks.
  - Picks are taken by reaching for them with grip, not by the hand
    rays: they are on or just in front of the player's own body. No
    reticle; a light tap says a hand is in reach. Kept by the
    maintainer after trying it.
  - TeaLeaf/LockFade shader: colour per point of the mesh, lit per
    point, never darker than a set floor so the lock can be seen in a
    dark room. Two materials from one shader: see-through only for
    the quarter second of the fade, solid the rest of the time.
  - The round lock plate on the door is cut by the same keyhole
    opening as the leaf (DoorKeyhole now takes extra renderers), so
    the door's keyway is keyhole-shaped; the big lock's is the plain
    rectangle.
  - Choices not in the spec, all kept: the big lock appears 0.2m out
    from the door and 0.3m below the head; a new pin is never within
    20 degrees of where the left pick already sits; stops 28 degrees
    apart.
  - Left over: chests, the lockpick finger pose (a copy of the rope
    grip for now), real sounds.
  - Lesson: the picks are made in Start(), not Awake().
    PlayerHandVisuals copies each hand model in its Awake() to make
    the ghost hands, and anything already attached to the hand would
    be copied onto the ghost.
  - Lesson: the hand model's axes, worked out from the scene and the
    snap profiles and now written down (Scripts/Player/CLAUDE.md):
    fingers along -Y, back of the hand -X, thumb side +Z. The first
    guesses at where the picks sit were right.
  - The new scripts were checked outside the editor with Unity's own
    compiler (Data/DotNetSdkRoslyn/csc.dll, run with the references
    and file list from the generated .csproj files) before the
    maintainer tested. Worth doing again for large changes.
- Door leftovers, all tested.
  - The body pushes an open door (the maintainer's decision).
    Player.PlayerBodyPushing uses the message Unity sends from inside
    CharacterController.Move() for each thing the body hits, and the
    same Push() the hands use. Unity only reports how far the body got
    before the hit, not how far it was trying to go, so the push is a
    set 1.5m a second.
  - A tap on the controller when a hand first touches a door it
    pushes (the maintainer's decision). A pushed door swings ahead and
    is caught up with again, so "first" means no push by that hand for
    half a second.
  - Sliding bolt (Interaction.DoorBolt): optional on any door, worked
    only from its own side, holds the door shut whatever the lock.
    Taken like a handle, slid by moving the hand along the door. Only
    shoots while the door is shut. The player's half is in
    PlayerHandDoors.
  - A placeholder creak, made from maths like the other placeholder
    sounds.
  - Sound portals on the test doorways, with a sound room each side
    of the wall and a knocking cube behind it: a shut door muffles,
    an open one doesn't.
  - Decided to leave for now: the door handle's hand pose.
- CLAUDE.md: the editor version corrected to 6000.3.25f1.
- Next: Phase 4 (loot, keys, inventory data, the wrist menu). Two

2026-10-09 (keyhole view, lockpicking design, a smithy built from code)
-----------------------------------------------------------------------
- Keyhole view (doors, step 3; Phase 3). Tried by the maintainer and
  working.
  - The roadmap's plan was a scope-style picture: a second camera at
    the keyhole drawing the far room into a small texture. That draws
    the room twice, against the Quest pixel rule. The maintainer chose
    the cheaper route instead: an opening that grows in the door itself
    as the head comes near, looked through with the player's own eyes.
    True depth in both eyes, the head looks round the room, nothing is
    drawn a second time. Not magnified.
  - TeaLeaf/DoorLeaf shader (Assets/Art/Shaders): a plain lit colour
    that throws away the pixels inside the keyhole's outline. The
    outline is a signed distance worked out per pixel. The shadow pass
    doesn't cut, so the door still casts a whole shadow. The edge is
    smoothed by sending coverage out as alpha (AlphaToMask).
  - Interaction.DoorKeyhole: an empty child of the door marking the
    keyhole. Tells the shader where the opening is (once) and how big
    (when it changes), through a MaterialPropertyBlock. Opens from
    0.6m to 0.25m of head distance, only while the door is latched.
    Only the picture changes: the door's collider is untouched.
  - Player.PlayerKeyholes: looks for a keyhole near the head five
    times a second and updates only that one each frame. New tick
    slot 7d, after Move().
  - Real depth, at the maintainer's request: the shader's cut is
    paper-thin, so DoorKeyhole also builds the opening's walls - a
    small tube mesh through the door's thickness, resized with the
    opening (same vertex array each time, no allocation).
  - Shape, at the maintainer's request: first a rounded rectangle, now
    a classic keyhole - a circle with a slot below it, the slot as
    wide as the circle's radius and a diameter long. One radius sizes
    it (5mm at rest, 5cm open). The shape is defined twice, in the
    shader (the cut) and in DoorKeyhole (the walls and gizmo), and the
    two must be kept matching.
  - Discussed, not built: a lock cylinder standing out from the door
    works if it's part of the door's mesh; as a separate object
    DoorKeyhole would need a list of renderers. The shader has no
    texture yet.
  - The Door Test Area builder gives the simple-lock door a keyhole
    and the DoorLeaf material, and adds PlayerKeyholes to the Player.
- Lockpicking redesigned by the maintainer (Phase 5 in the roadmap
  rewritten; not built). Two picks worn on the back of the left hand,
  grabbed as one by the right and snapped into a simple lock's
  keyhole; a large copy of the lock then fades in front of the door
  with a pick at 11 and 1 o'clock. The right hand turns its pick
  clockwise through three stops and a last turn; at each stop the
  left hand sweeps its pick between 11 and 7 o'clock to find a random
  pin by haptics (rising from 10 degrees out, full within 5), holding
  it for a second. Letting go of the right pick resets. A hand turns a
  pick by moving round the lock, not twisting the wrist. Unlocked
  until the level restarts. Walking away puts the picks back on the
  hand. This replaces the single-pick wrist-roll plan; the picks are
  no longer on the wrist radial menu (Phase 4 line changed).
- Cobblestone shader: a crevice shadow, standing in for ambient
  occlusion, after the maintainer found the step from stone to gap
  hard and the gaps unlit-looking. Colour and shine are darkened most
  on the line where stone meets gap, easing off both ways; narrow gaps
  stay dark, wide pockets lighten in the middle. Two new settings
  (Crevice Shadow, Crevice Shadow Width). Not added to the brick
  shader. No word yet on how it looks.
- Smithy (TeaLeaf > Build Smithy), from a reference picture the

2026-10-08 (doors, a fog test, cobblestone and brick wall shaders)
------------------------------------------------------------------
- Doors, started (Phase 3). The maintainer's design: three kinds of
  lock (none, simple = can be picked, keyed); any unlocked door opens
  by its handle; the handle is ray-targeted and grabbed like a ledge
  and the hand snaps onto it; turning the hand 60 degrees frees the
  door; it swings either way; let go near closed, it shuts again;
  an open door is moved by the hands touching it; a simple-lock door
  shows a larger view through its keyhole when the head is close; any
  door may have a sliding bolt worked only from its own side.
  Built in steps:
  - Step 1 - the door itself. Interaction.Door (on the hinge),
    DoorHandle (a hand target and snap target), DoorLock, and
    Player.PlayerHandDoors on the Hands object. Decisions made while
    building, for the maintainer to overrule:
    - The angle is set from code on a kinematic Rigidbody, with no
      hinge joint or forces: exact under a snapped hand, cheap, and it
      still shoves loose props aside.
    - Locked and latched are two separate states. A locked door's
      lever turns 10 degrees, stops and rattles (with a haptic knock).
    - The twist is the controller's turn about the handle's spindle,
      measured relative to the door, so a swinging door isn't a twist.
    - The swing is the change in the hand's bearing round the hinge
      each frame, so the door turns as far as the hand went round at
      any distance from the hinge.
    - The door never swings into the player's body (one box check
      against the Player layer before each move), and the hand is let
      go if the real hand gets 0.4m from the handle.
    - One hand per door; one handle serves both sides, the side
      chosen by where the head is, as a two-sided ledge does.
    - The Door component disables itself whenever it's at rest, so a
      door costs nothing until it moves.
    - A hand does one thing: climbing and carrying skip a hand that's
      on a door, and the door skips a hand that's climbing or carrying.
    New tick slots: playerHandDoors.Tick() after carrying, and
    TickHeld() straight before the hand visuals on every path.
  - Step 2 - hands push an open door. The physical hands' sweep looks
    up whatever stopped it in a new HandPushRegistry; an IHandPushable
    (the door's leaf) is told where it was pressed and how far the
    hand was still trying to go, and moves itself. The door keeps the
    speed it was moved at and swings on, slowing down - also after
    being let go of while moving. A door swinging by itself latches
    when it reaches closed; it only goes through its frame while a
    hand takes it through.
  - Bug: Auto Sync Transforms is off in the project, so physics only
    learns a collider has moved at its next step. The hands sweep
    every frame, and would have kept pushing a door that had already
    moved. The door now calls Physics.SyncTransforms() after each
    move.
  - Bug, found in the headset: the hand stuttered while pushing. It
    was stopped at the door's surface each frame, which counts as
    contact - rotation held, then the 0.1s ease back - so it flipped
    between held and easing back every frame or two. Fix: Push()
    returns whether the door moved, and if it did the hand's sweep is
    tried again in the same frame, so a pushing hand stays on its
    controller. Written, not yet confirmed in the headset. A side
    effect: no contact haptic when a door is pushed.
  - Left: the keyhole view, the sliding bolt, keys, a creak sound, a
    hand pose for the handle (a copy of the bottle's for now), a sound
    portal on a real doorway. In the roadmap.
  - TeaLeaf > Build Door Test Area: a wall with one door of each lock
    kind. Right-click a Door for Test Lock / Test Unlock.
- Fog test (temporary). Core/Debug/FogTestDebug: a Play Mode toggle
  for Silent Hill 2 style fog, so the maintainer can judge the look.
  Unity's built-in fog (not a full-screen effect: each lit shader
  blends itself towards the fog colour by distance). Then two extras
  the maintainer asked about:
  - A sky gradient: the stars stay, and a fog-coloured dome round the
    head is drawn over them, solid at the horizon and thinning
    overhead.
  - Thinner fog indoors: a ray straight up from the head a few times
    a second; a roof overhead eases the fog down to a tenth.
  What was learned: built-in fog is one global setting. It can't tell
  indoors from out - seen from inside, the street through a window
  clears too - and it goes by distance, not height, so a tall distant
  building is fogged to its top against a sky that has cleared. Doing
  either properly needs the project's own lit shader for level
  geometry (fog per surface, thinning with height). No verdict yet:
  the maintainer is thinking about it. The component stays until then.
- Cobblestone shader (TeaLeaf/Cobblestone), at the maintainer's
  request: a cobbled floor with no textures. Each pixel works out
  which stone it's on from its position in the world, so the cobbles
  are the same size on any object, floor pieces join without a seam,
  and nothing repeats. Rounded irregular stones (a Voronoi pattern,
  with the borders blended by a smooth minimum to round the corners),
  a colour per stone from a dark red / brown / grey range, a gap
  colour, lighting from the height's change between pixels (no normal
  map), optional one-shift parallax, and a fade to a flat colour in
  the distance so it doesn't shimmer. Then, as the maintainer tuned
  it:
  - A dirt layer: grime patches, mottled stones, gritty gaps, ragged
    edges, and a warp of the whole pattern.
  - Size variation. A hexagon grid was suggested and wouldn't have
    helped - still one stone per cell. Instead each stone gets a
    random weight and heavier stones take ground from lighter ones (a
    power diagram). The border distance stays exact, so the gaps keep
    their width between stones of any size.
  - The maintainer found a high Corner Roundness (0.5) gave the
    shape wanted (a separate oval outline was discussed and not needed).
    That opened large pockets where stones meet, so Gap Width can now
    go negative, with a Minimum Gap line always kept between
    neighbours so they don't fuse.
  - Bug: the dirt only showed close to the camera. The fine noise
    was faded out once sub-pixel, with fixed numbers and no slider,
    and the maintainer's flecks were 5mm. Now the flecks double in
    size with distance instead (as often as needed, blended), so
    there's always some texture and still no shimmer. Later: fleck
    size down to 0.5mm, and the pixel limit as a slider.
  - Bug: thin straight lines across the stones up close, with
    wrongly lit wedges beside them. I got this wrong twice. I assumed
    a tiny step where a far stone drops out of the 3x3 cells searched,
    and tried to fade far stones' influence out: first by distance,
    which took the rounding off every stone (a stone's ordinary
    neighbours are a cell away); then near the cell edge, which made
    the lines worse. Both reverted. The maintainer's two tests (lines
    stay with Dirt off, vanish at Lighting Strength 0) and a
    screenshot showed the real cause: with size variation the stones
    are well over a cell across, and 3x3 cells don't contain all of a
    big stone's neighbours, so borders go missing. Fix: search 5x5 by
    default, with a Narrow Search toggle back to 3x3. Confirmed gone.
    Lesson: don't hide missing neighbours, search far enough to have
    them - and get a screenshot before a second guess.
- Brick wall shader (TeaLeaf/BrickWall): the same idea for brick and
  coursed stone. Rows with every other one slid along, rectangular
  bricks with rounded corners and a bevel, sizes in metres, optional
  variation in length, row height and row offset (for stone), and each
  brick's face leaning a slightly different way. A pixel's brick is
  found directly, with no search, so it's far cheaper than the
  cobbles. The courses run level along the wall whichever way it
  faces. It shares its noise, lighting and extra passes with the
  cobble shader by copying, not an include - a fix to one needs
  making in both.
- Performance, said at each step and still true: both shaders are
  paid per pixel on surfaces that fill the view, which is the
  tightest budget on Quest. The cobbles now search 25 cells a pixel
  (50 with parallax). Fine for judging the look through PCVR; they
  need profiling on the device, and probably baking to textures,
  before they go on a real level. Neither has a pass for the
  lightmapper yet. Both in the roadmap (Phase 10).

2026-10-07 (fifth part - performance rules and a settings review)
-----------------------------------------------------------------
- Started as questions, no code: normal maps against parallax maps,
  Silent Hill's fog as a way to shorten the draw distance, and a list
  of the performance tricks used since the start of 3D graphics.
  What came out of it for this game:
  - A normal map only changes how a flat surface is lit; parallax
    shifts where the texture is read, so it shows real depth. The
    convincing kind (parallax occlusion) takes many texture reads per
    pixel - too much for Quest on walls and floors, which fill the
    view. In VR two eyes show a normal-mapped wall is flat up close,
    and a few real polygons are cheap. So: model the depth the player
    gets near, keep normal maps for fine detail.
  - Fog saves nothing itself. It hides the edge of the world so the
    far clip plane can come in, and that saves draw calls and
    vertices - not pixels, which are the tight budget. Indoors, walls
    already do the job. And this game has stars: fogged buildings
    would stand as flat shapes against them unless the sky fades into
    the same haze at the horizon. Kept in reserve as thin haze, not
    planned.
- The maintainer's rule from today: everything built from here on is
  designed against the performance list, not fixed up in Phase 10.
  Written into the root CLAUDE.md as "Performance rules (Quest 3)":
  pixels first (no full-screen effects, no many-sample shaders on
  big surfaces), cheap batchable shaders, baked light with few
  unshadowed realtime lights, static level geometry on shared
  materials, modelled depth, layouts that break long sightlines,
  costly CPU work sliced over time, nothing created during play.

2026-10-07 (fourth part - procedural night sky)
-----------------------------------------------
- The panoramic photo skybox is gone (the maintainer didn't like it):
  qwantani_night_puresky_4k.exr (73 MB) and the NightSky material are
  deleted. In its place Core.ProceduralSky, on the Directional Light,
  makes the sky when the level loads - in OnEnable(), before the first
  frame, so already behind the level-start black. The sky is a picture
  only: nothing in it lights the scene; that stays with the Directional
  Light and the ambient setting.
- First version, replaced the same day: a generated cubemap (1024 per
  face) of stars and nebula clouds, with the moon drawn by the skybox
  shader. Seamless, because every pixel was worked out from its
  direction alone, and it could be saved as a cached file. Seen in the
  editor: the stars were too soft. A cubemap pixel at 1024 is about
  0.11 degrees and a Quest 3 pixel about 0.04, so a star is always at
  least a blurred cubemap pixel; 2048 would take 96 MB and still be
  soft. Lesson: points of light don't belong in a texture. The
  generator, its settings and the cache were removed, not kept.
- Second version, the one committed: no skybox at all. The camera
  clears to black and the stars are geometry - one mesh, one draw call,
  a quad per star, the round shape and a one-pixel smooth edge worked
  out in the shader (TeaLeaf/Sky). The mesh only stores each star's
  direction, colour and size; the shader places the corners 1m from the
  eye in that direction and pushes the depth to the far plane. So the
  stars are locked to the camera: no parallax, the same direction in
  both eyes (which is what infinity looks like in stereo), and the far
  clip distance doesn't matter. Drawn after solid geometry, so stars
  behind a building are rejected by the depth test.
- No star is made below the horizon (they'd never be seen): directions
  are picked on the upper half of the sphere only, fading in over the
  first few degrees (horizonFade) so they don't stop at a line.
- Twinkle, in the shader, per vertex: each star's brightness wavers by
  three sine waves at unrelated speeds, with its own starting point and
  speed, strongest at the horizon and least overhead. No script work
  per frame.
- Against shimmer: a star under a pixel wide flickers as the head
  moves, so none is drawn smaller than minStarPixels (1.5) - it's drawn
  that size and dimmed by as much as it was enlarged.
- Moon: a second quad, turned to where the Directional Light shines
  from (the same direction Moonlight uses), drawn after the stars so it
  covers them. One blend mode does both jobs: alpha 0 adds (stars),
  alpha 1 covers (moon).
- Moon surface (Core.MoonSurfaceBuilder): the moon's face is painted
  once at level load into a 512 texture that the shader multiplies the
  moon's colour by - dark seas, craters of many sizes (largest first,
  so small ones lie over big ones; darker floor, bright rim, one side
  of the rim lit), a couple of craters with ray streaks, mottling and a
  darker edge. Each pixel is first turned into a point on a ball, and
  everything is worked out there, so craters near the edge are squashed
  as on the real moon. Painted ahead of time rather than computed per
  pixel in the shader because a texture has mipmaps, which stop crater
  detail finer than a screen pixel from flickering. Burst jobs, a few
  milliseconds. Flat lit, as a full moon; no phases.
- Shader notes: the moon's version of the shader is a keyword
  (_SURFACE_TEXTURE) declared with multi_compile, not shader_feature -
  the moon's material is made in code, and a build strips shader_feature
  versions that no saved material uses. And a Burst job mustn't touch a
  class's managed static fields, so the builder has none.
- Tuning: every setting is on the component and the sky is rebuilt as
  they're changed, in Edit Mode too. The Scene view keeps its own
  background, so judge the black in the Game view or the headset.
- State: compiles, and Unity's log shows no shader or Burst errors. The
  cubemap version was seen in the editor; the star-quad sky, the
  twinkle and the moon surface are not yet confirmed in the editor or
  the headset. To check there: star size and shimmer, twinkle strength,
  and whether the moon's craters read at 4 degrees.
- Not built: nebula clouds, left for later. The route agreed is a small
  cloud-only cubemap (about 256 per face) behind the stars - clouds are
  soft, so low resolution wouldn't show.
- The scene's default reflection was baked from the old sky and stays
  until lighting is regenerated.
- Roadmap: no change - the sky isn't one of its items.


2026-10-07 (third part - impact noise)
--------------------------------------
- Impact noise built (Interaction.ImpactNoise), tested, works. A prop
  with it sounds when it hits something: one call to the sound player
  plays the impact for the player and emits the noise for guards, at
  the point of contact. On the prop's Rigidbody object, separate from
  Grabbable, so anything physical can have one. Nothing per frame -
  all of it is in OnCollisionEnter().
- How hard it hit is the collision's impulse over the prop's mass: the
  speed the collision took off the prop. Chosen over the speed the two
  were closing at, because a prop sliding from one floor piece onto the
  next starts a new collision without losing any speed, and would have
  sounded. Below 1 m/s silent (a prop settling, or set down gently);
  from there to 8 m/s the volume goes from a quarter to full and the
  distance guards hear it from goes from 30% to the cue's whole radius.
- What it hit: SurfaceSounds.GetLoudness(surface), new - the surface's
  footstep noise radius over stone's (carpet about 0.4, wood 1.2, metal
  1.7). So a surface's loudness is still tuned in one place, its
  footstep cue; the cost is that impacts and footsteps can't be tuned
  apart per surface. A soft surface also dulls the sound (the sound
  player's muffle), not just quietens it.
- Kept from repeating: at most one sound per prop every 0.15s (a prop
  bouncing or rattling to rest starts many collisions a second), and
  two props hitting each other sound once - both are told about the
  same collision in the same physics step, so the second is skipped if
  it's in the same step and within 0.3m of the last one played.
- A carried prop makes no impacts: it's out of physics while held.
- One cue per prop for every surface so far (the placeholder impact) -
  a bottle sounds the same on wood as on stone but for volume and
  dullness. Sounds per surface would be a later step.
- TeaLeaf > Add Grabbable Test Props now gives each prop an ImpactNoise
  with the placeholder cue and surface sounds. Main.unity as committed
  does not have them: the scene wasn't saved after testing.
- Correction to the entry below: the arc's dots were retuned to 0.6cm
  radius, 30cm apart.
- Roadmap: impact noise ticked in Phase 3.


2026-10-07 (continued - aimed throwing, hand smoothing)
-------------------------------------------------------
- Aimed throwing built (Player.PlayerHandThrowing, on Hands), tested,
  works. With a prop fully in hand, pulling that hand's trigger past 0.6
  shows the arc the prop will fly along and a disc where it lands;
  letting the trigger out to 0.3 throws it along exactly that arc. Two
  trigger values with a gap, so a hovering finger can't flicker between
  aiming and throwing. Every throw leaves at 9 m/s, so the hand's tilt
  sets the distance. Runs at tick step 8a, after carried props, so the
  arc starts from where the prop is this frame.
- Cancel is aiming at nothing, as designed: tilted above 75 or below -60
  degrees, no landing within 2.5s of flight, or something right in
  front of the hand - the arc goes red and faded with no disc, and
  letting go of the trigger keeps the prop. Letting go of grip while
  aiming is still the ordinary drop or physical throw.
- The launch is a short move of the hand, not an animation clip (the
  roadmap said animation): the hand visual is snapped 15cm forward
  along the throw over 0.08s with HandVisualSnap - the same thing that
  snaps a hand onto a ledge - lets the prop go at the end, and comes
  back over 0.2s. The arc is drawn from where that move ends, so the
  prop leaves from the start of the line the player saw. The body's
  velocity is not added (unlike the physical throw): it has to land
  where shown.
- The arc uses Unity's gravity, not the textbook's. Physics moves in
  steps and adds gravity to the speed before each move, so a body falls
  t x (t + step) where the formula says t squared - about 10cm lower a
  second into the flight. Drawing with the same sum puts the line where
  the prop really goes.
- After an aimed throw the hand stays busy until grip is let go. Grip
  is still held then (the trigger threw it), and a held grip picks up
  or climbs whatever the hand ray is on - so the hand would have
  grabbed the next thing at once.
- Change (maintainer): the aim points straight out of the front of the
  controller, like a menu pointer. First built on the hand rays'
  direction, which is angled out from the palm for grabbing (12 degrees
  down, 70 sideways) - wrong for pointing at a distance.
- The arc looked too coarse (one piece per 0.05s of flight), and making
  it finer meant up to 150 rays a frame, one per piece. The maintainer
  asked whether there was a cheaper way. There was: finding where it
  lands and drawing it are two jobs. The landing is now found with a
  few long pieces (0.1s each, at most 25 rays) - a straight piece cuts
  the curve's corner by only gravity x step squared / 8, about 1cm -
  and the line is drawn from the formula alone, as finely as wanted,
  for almost nothing. Lesson: the rays were never for drawing.
- Arc style toggle (maintainer's request): a line (LineRenderer) or
  dots. The dots are one mesh of up to 256 octagons, one every so many
  centimetres of distance along the path, each turned to face the head;
  its vertex array is made once and rewritten each frame of aiming, so
  no objects per dot and nothing allocated. Player.ThrowArc draws
  either, with the landing disc; one per hand, made at runtime. The
  maintainer's settings: dots, 24cm apart, a 2cm landing disc.
- Hand smoothing built, tested, works. The tracking is accurate enough
  that the real shake of a hand showed in game. Fix: a One Euro filter
  (Player.HandPoseFilter) - simple smoothing whose strength falls as
  the hand speeds up, so a still hand is steadied and a moving one
  isn't lagged. Two numbers each for position and rotation: how much a
  still hand is smoothed, and how fast that's given up with speed.
  (Named for being cheap: a 2012 paper, "1 euro filter".)
- Where it goes: PlayerTracking.Tick(), first thing each frame, writes
  the smoothed pose over the one the Tracked Pose Drivers put on the
  controller objects. Everything that follows those objects - hand
  models, rays, throw arc, carried props, ghost hands - is steadied
  with no changes of its own. It only works because the hands' drivers
  were set to "Update" on the 4th; if they moved again before
  rendering, the raw pose would come back. Smoothed in tracking space,
  not the world, so the body walking or turning isn't hand movement.
- Tuned per device: a set of numbers for PCVR and one for Quest
  standalone (HandSmoothingSettings), chosen by what the game is
  running on, or forced to try the Quest's numbers in the editor. On
  the PlayerTracking component - no scene setup. Still the first
  guesses (PCVR 3 Hz / 40 / 3 Hz / 15, Quest 5 Hz / 40 / 5 Hz / 15).
- Climbing uses the smoothed hands too (maintainer's decision). First
  built with climbing on the raw tracked position, on the reasoning
  that it moves the body by exactly how far the hand moved; but that
  is also why a shaking hand on a ledge shakes the whole view. The raw
  position accessors were removed again. Tested in the headset, works.
- A working rule from the maintainer: when a prompt ends with a
  question, answer it and build nothing.
- Roadmap: aimed throwing ticked in Phase 3.

  so a hand held against a wall throws nothing.
- Physical throwing built (PlayerHandHolding), tested, works. Each
  frame a prop is carried, the hand notes where the prop's middle is,
  which way the hand is turned, and the time, in a ring of 32 slots per
  hand made once (nothing allocated per frame). On letting go: velocity
  = the distance between the newest note and the oldest one within the
  last 0.1s, over the time between them; spin from the turn between the
  two. Below 1 m/s it's a drop from rest, as before. Details that
  mattered:
  - Measured at the prop's middle, not the wrist, so a wrist flick
    counts.
  - Measured relative to the rig, with the body's own velocity added
    back at the throw: in the world, a snap turn swings the hand
    sideways in one frame and would read as a huge throw. Adding the
    body back means a throw while running goes further, and a hand
    stopped on a wall while walking cancels to nothing.
  - Nothing is noted until the hand is back at the controller after a
    pick-up, or letting go at once would throw the prop at the player
    with the return's speed.
  - Capped at 12 m/s against tracking glitches. A thrown prop switches
    to continuous collision detection so it can't pass through a thin
    wall between physics steps; its own setting comes back at the next
    pick-up (a kinematic body doesn't support it).
  The four tuning values (window, minimum speed, strength, cap) are
  still the first guesses - they felt right.
- Bug: thrown and dropped props looked like a low frame rate, though the
  movement was right. Physics moves bodies 50 times a second (Fixed
  Timestep 0.02) and the headset draws 72-120 frames, so a prop sat
  still for a frame or two, then jumped. Fix: Rigidbody interpolation,
  set from code - Interpolate when let go, None while held (it's placed
  through its parent then, and interpolation would pull it back towards
  where physics last had it). Lesson: it's a setting on each Rigidbody,
  not the project; lowering the Fixed Timestep instead would make all
  physics cost more and only match one refresh rate.
- Change (maintainer's request): a carried prop pushes nothing, like the
  hand visual. It was a kinematic body, and a kinematic body still
  shoves any physics body it's moved into. Fix: Rigidbody
  .detectCollisions off while carried. It still stops at things, because
  that was never its collider - the hand's sweep covers the prop.
- Bug that fix caused: take the bottom prop from a stack and the others
  hung in the air. A body at rest is asleep - not simulated at all,
  gravity included - until something touches it, and switching
  collisions off isn't a touch. Fix: Grabbable.WakeNeighbours() wakes
  every body within 5cm of the prop as it leaves.
- Then moved to the right moment (maintainer's request): the prop goes
  out of physics when the hand reaches it (Grabbable.BeginCarry(),
  called from FinishReach()), not at the start of the reach, so a stack
  falls as the prop is lifted rather than 0.12s early. During the reach

2026-10-04 (continued - visibility and the night look)
------------------------------------------------------
- Visibility (Phase 2), and how the design changed twice in a day. Built
  first as the roadmap said: designer-placed shadow volumes in a lit
  world. The maintainer pointed out the levels are at night, so it was
  turned round - light volumes in a dark world, moonlight as a low-level
  volume. Then the better idea: the editor already shows shadows cast by
  the geometry, so use those, and place nothing but the lights. That's
  what was kept. Neither volume version was ever placed in the scene.
- How it works. The rendered shadows can't be read cheaply (they live on
  the graphics card), so the same question is asked with a physics ray:
  from a point towards the moon - does it hit anything? Core.SceneLight
  .LevelAt(point) returns the brightest light reaching a point: moonlight
  (Core.Moonlight, on the Directional Light, level 0.4) unless geometry
  blocks the ray, and each Core.LightSource (torch, lamp: level, range,
  full brightness out to half the range then fading) unless out of range
  or blocked. Rays hit Environment + Interactable, so walls, roofs, props
  and closed doors all block light. Disabling a LightSource puts it out.
  The catch: gameplay shadows only match the rendered ones where the
  shadow-casting mesh has a collider.
- Player.PlayerVisibility (tick step 7c, also during a mantle): one
  Visibility value, 0 hidden to 1 plain sight. Light is sampled at the
  head and the middle of the body, averaged, ten times a second (each
  sample is a few rays); every frame it's multiplied by stance (crouch
  0.6, sprint 1.3) and eased, so a shadow's edge fades rather than
  flickers. Moonlight gives 0.4 standing, 0.24 crouched - turning that
  into "seen from afar" against "only close up" is the guards' vision
  range, Phase 6.
- VisibilityDebug: a head-locked gauge drawn through DebugLines (bar =
  visibility, tick = raw light level), standing in for the wrist gem.
- Sound Test House: three light sources with matching point lights (Hall
  and Kitchen torches, a Lounge lamp); the moon does the rest - Yard and
  roofless upper floor moonlit, ground floor dark away from the torches,
  Store dark behind its closed door.
- The night look (done in the editor by the maintainer): Directional

2026-10-04 (surfaces and footsteps)
-----------------------------------
- Surfaces (Phase 2). Core.SurfaceType (Stone, Wood, Carpet, Metal, Water,
  then Tile, Grass and Gravel added the same day - new ones go at the end,
  since tags are saved as the number). Core.SurfaceTag marks a floor, on
  the collider or any parent, so one tag on a group covers a whole floor;
  anything untagged is stone. Chosen over a physics-material lookup for
  that reason. Core.SurfaceSounds is one asset listing each surface's
  footstep cue. A surface's loudness to guards is simply its cue's noise
  radius, so audio and noise are tuned in one place as for other sounds.
- Footsteps (Phase 2). Player.PlayerFootsteps, tick step 7b (straight
  after TickState). A step is a distance, not a timer: real horizontal
  movement is added up and a step sounds each time it reaches the gait's
  stride (walk 1.4m, sprint 1.6m, crouch 1.2m), so pushing into a wall is
  silent. Standing still keeps the distance so far - dropping it would
  let short bursts of movement cross a room in silence. Loudness =
  surface x stance x speed: each gait has a noise and a volume scale, and
  a part-pushed stick gives quieter steps (average speed over the stride
  against the gait's full speed, down to 0.4). The surface is found only
  when a foot comes down: one ray, SurfaceTag.TryFindBelow().
- Landings. First built with a landing cue per surface (a generated
  thud); replaced the same day, maintainer's decision: a landing is the
  surface's own step sound played loud, twice, 0.1s apart - one foot then
  the other. One noise event for the whole landing (type Landing, 1.5x a
  walking step's radius at a flat jump, scaled by fall speed).
- SoundPlayer.Play() gained three optional parameters: volumeScale,
  delay (AudioSource.PlayDelayed - the audio engine does the waiting, so
  two-part sounds need no timers; the first landing version had its own
  timer in Tick and had to handle a mantle interrupting it) and muffle
  (extra dulling of one sound: filter only, not volume).
- Real footstep clips. A CC-BY/CC0 footstep pack added under
  Assets/Audio/footsteps (a license.txt in each folder; credit needed in
  a release). The cues now play boots (stone), wood, metal, water, tile,
  grass and gravel; carpet uses the grass clips until a real one turns
  up. The first five cues kept their "Placeholder" names because the test
  builders load them by path. The three new cues and the edits to the
  existing ones were written straight into the .asset files (with .meta
  files and guids made by hand) - Unity imported them without complaint.
- The stand-in guard now walks on surfaces: SoundEmitterDebug takes an
  optional SurfaceSounds and plays the step for the floor under it. The
  Sound Test House gained floors for every surface (wood upstairs and
  stairs, carpet Lounge, tile Kitchen, metal Store; puddle, gravel path
  and lawn in the Yard).
- Heel and toe (maintainer's idea, for hard surfaces). Per surface in
  SurfaceSounds: a toggle, the share of steps that get it, the toe's
  volume relative to the heel, the delay and a muffle amount. The step
  plays as normal, then the same cue again, quieter and duller, a moment
  later. Which steps get it is not a dice roll (that gives long runs):
  HeelAndToeCadence adds the ratio to a credit each step and fires when
  it passes a target re-picked at random around 1, so the average is the
  ratio, a step early or late. Tried the other way round (quiet heel,
  normal toe): rejected by ear. What made it work was muffling the toe.
  On for stone (tuned by ear: ratio 0.71, toe volume 0.05, delay 0.08s,
  muffle 0.5) and tile.
- Lesson: when a field is renamed, [FormerlySerializedAs] keeps values
  already tuned in the Inspector; and editing an asset on disk while the
  Inspector holds unsaved changes to it risks losing them - new fields
  were left at zero for the maintainer to set instead.


2026-10-03 (third part - noise and sound)
-----------------------------------------
- Headset tests passed for last session's work: the caught/win/restart
  loop and the end-fade freeze, the ladder mantle and both zip lines.
  Ticked in the roadmap.
- Noise events (Phase 2). Core.Noise (a struct: position, radius, type,
  source), NoiseType, INoiseListener and the static NoiseSystem:
  Emit() tells every registered listener the noise reaches. Nothing per
  frame, no scene search, no allocation. An Emitted event is raised for
  every noise, heard or not, for debug drawing and UI. SoundCue is one
  asset for one kind of sound, holding both halves: the audio (clips,
  volume, pitch variation, audible range) and the noise (type, radius).
  How far the player hears a sound and how far guards react to it are
  separate values, tuned in one place.
- Sound design, and how it changed. The aim: a sound should tell the
  player truthfully where it is on stereo headphones (in Thief VR, guard
  sounds had little distance or blocking processing, and a guard on
  another floor is confusing with no up/down cue). First plan: sound
  zones linked by hand (same zone clear, linked muffled, unlinked silent)
  plus ray checks to muffle anything behind geometry. Replaced the same
  day by Thief's own idea, rooms and portals, which does the same job
  better: the floor above is heard from the stairwell, not just switched
  off. The in-room ray checks were then dropped - with portals handling
  walls, a guard in the same room should simply be clear; position and
  reverb matter more there. Roadmap Phase 2 gained a sound system section.
- Rooms and portals. SoundRoom is a maths box like ExitZone; anywhere not
  in a room is "outside", one big room of its own. SoundPortal is a
  rectangle in a doorway, stairwell or window; it finds the room on each
  side by probing a short way out from each face. SoundPropagation gives
  the route between two points: straight in the same room, otherwise the
  shortest way through portals, or nothing. The cost is paid ahead of
  time - a table of shortest routes between every pair of portals
  (Floyd-Warshall), rebuilt only when the layout or a door changes - so a
  query is a few additions, no physics. Sound arrives from the point of
  the last portal nearest the straight line, so a source seen through an
  open doorway is heard exactly where it is. A closed portal adds muffle
  and extra distance; one that never opens is a thin wall or window.
  Walls never leak by themselves. NoiseSystem uses the same routes, so
  guards hear by path distance and are told where the noise seemed to
  come from (the doorway to turn towards).
- SoundPlayer. A pool of 16 AudioSources made once; Play(cue, position)
  emits the cue's noise and plays its audio along the route: from the
  direction of the portal, placed as far away as the whole route, so
  Unity's own panning and distance fade follow the route. The low-pass
  filter is switched off on voices that aren't muffled. One-shots only so
  far. Placeholder sounds are generated from maths (TeaLeaf > Create
  Placeholder Sounds): footstep and impact .wav files and their cues.
- Tuning from listening. Sounds faded in noticeably when moving fast: the
  fade was a straight line to silence at a short range, and routes are
  longer than they look. Ranges raised (footsteps 12m to 25m, impact 20m
  to 35m) and the fade changed to a curve, (1 - distance/range) squared,
  which drops fast close up and slowly far away. Then a mild muffle for
  every open portal a sound passes (Open Muffle 0.3, adding up), except a
  source heard in a straight line through an open doorway.
- Test areas and debug. TeaLeaf > Build Sound Test Area (three rooms, a
  closed portal) and Build Sound Test House (two storeys, stairwell
  portal lying flat in the stair hole, closed door, window, a walled yard
  as its own room, a stand-in guard walking Bedroom to Kitchen and back).
  NoiseDebug draws each noise as a fading sphere; NoiseListenerDebug is
  a stand-in pair of ears; SoundEmitterDebug repeats a cue;
  WaypointMoverDebug walks a route. N and L write a one-entry report to
  the Console (rooms, straight and route distances, muffle, heard or
  not), which can be read back from Editor.log - much quicker than
  judging cubes in the headset. All verified in headset.
- Portal links in the editor. Asked whether to bake each portal's rooms
  in the editor: decided not to - the lookup costs well under a
  millisecond once per layout change, and baked links would go stale
  silently when something is moved. Instead a door opening or closing no
  longer redoes the lookup (MarkLayoutDirty vs MarkDirty), SoundRoom is
  ExecuteAlways so rooms are known in Edit Mode, a portal with the same
  room on both sides is drawn yellow, and its Inspector names the two
  rooms it joins (SoundPortalEditor).
- Bugs and lessons. The first NoiseListenerDebug only drew a wire marker,

  several new files and logged errors for a class not yet written:

- Design only, no code: aimed throwing added as a slice mechanic. Instead
  of physically throwing a held object, grip holds it, holding that hand's
  trigger shows a trajectory arc, and releasing the trigger plays a short
  hand visual launch animation and throws the object along the arc.
  Distance comes from the hand's pitch with a fixed launch speed, like
  standard VR teleport arcs. The aim is accuracy (noisemakers and
  distractions need to land where intended) and avoiding how awkward

  pushed forward and threw on a button still to be chosen. That clashed

  both - either hand throws what it holds, and the sticks aren't involved.
  The triggers only curled the index finger until now, so they were free.

  up or down, or an arc with no valid landing, turns the arc red/faded,
